# Terminal Value

### What Death Was Doing for the Economy, and What Happens When We Stop It

**Format:** 13 chapters. Roughly 35,000 words of body text, about 160 printed pages at 6 x 9.
**Register:** Serious trade nonfiction. Argument driven, sourced, no equations in the body. Technical material goes in the endnotes.
**Voice:** First person, rationed. No more than twice per chapter, only at the point of an analytical claim, never autobiographical.
**Style rules:** No dashes. Short sentences. Every abstract claim followed within a paragraph by something concrete.

---

## The argument in one paragraph

Economics treats death as a boundary condition and never inspects it. But death is quietly doing at least five jobs. It sets the discount rate, it turns over capital, it turns over ideas, it prices risk, and it creates vacancy. Each one is load bearing, and none of them has a backup. Radical life extension removes all five at once. The result is not utopia and not catastrophe but something stranger: a civilization that is wealthy, safe, static and permanently locked in. Meanwhile artificial agents arrive as the first economic actors with genuinely arbitrary lifespans, and the turnover function migrates from people to machines. We become the immortal class that holds the capital. They become the mortal class that takes the risk, absorbs the churn, and settles the frontier. The book's constructive claim is that any society which abolishes death has to deliberately rebuild, by institution, everything death used to do by accident.

# PART I. THE UNEXAMINED CONSTANT

## 1. The Boundary Condition

**Argument:** Every economic model contains a hidden constant. The agents die. Finite lifespans are assumed so deeply that the field has built machinery to suppress what happens without them, rather than asking what the suppression is telling us.

**The hook:** Infinite horizon models misbehave. Left alone they generate bubbles, Ponzi paths and infinite valuations. Economists rule this out by imposing something called a transversality condition, which is a technical assumption that the value of what you are holding eventually goes to zero. It is a patch. My claim is that the problem it patches over was never a modeling artifact. It is a preview. It is showing us what an economy of people who do not die actually does, and we have been waving it away for seventy years.

**Also here:** Samuelson's overlapping generations model from 1958, an entire framework whose engine is that people die and get replaced. Introduce the five jobs as the spine of the book.

**Evidence needed:** Samuelson 1958. Standard treatment of transversality conditions. Blanchard's perpetual youth model from 1985, which handles mortality as a constant hazard rate and is the closest existing formal tool to what this book needs.

---

## 2. Assume a Longer Life

**Argument:** Set the premise honestly and conservatively. This is the chapter that decides whether serious readers stay.

**The move:** Refuse the hype. The argument needs no immortality, no uploading, no particular biotechnology. It needs only healthy lifespans in the range of 150 to 300 years, which is a far weaker claim, and every consequence in this book follows at that level. State the timeline uncertainty plainly, then set it aside. This is a book about what the economics does if, not a forecast of when.

**Also here:** The difference between lifespan and healthspan, and why only healthspan matters economically. A hundred extra years of frailty changes nothing. A hundred extra years of capacity changes everything.

**Evidence needed:** The current demographic trajectory and its slowdown. The Gompertz curve introduced properly here so chapter 6 can use it. An honest survey of where the biology actually stands, with the promotional material discounted.

# PART II. THE FIVE JOBS

## 3. The Price of Time

**Job one. Death sets the discount rate.**

**Argument:** In the standard formulation, the discount rate contains a mortality term. Part of why the future is worth less than the present is the chance you will not be there to collect. Reduce mortality and the rate falls automatically, with no policy decision and no change in anyone's preferences.

**The consequence:** As the rate approaches zero, the present value of any permanent asset runs away to infinity. Land, water rights, orbital slots, spectrum, anything that lasts forever stops having a finite price. This is not a curiosity. It is the valuation regime the entire capital stack sits on, and it does not survive.

**The empirical hook, and the best one in the book:** Real interest rates across the developed world have been falling for forty years, and the demographic explanation is already mainstream. We are not speculating about a future mechanism. We are watching a mild early version of it and filing it under monetary policy. This is where the practitioner's chair earns its keep.

**Evidence needed:** Ramsey 1928 and the modern Ramsey rule. Blanchard 1985 for the mortality term. The secular stagnation and demographic savings literature. Japan as the furthest along case. The Stern and Nordhaus dispute over climate discount rates, which is the one place this argument is already being had in public.

---

## 4. The Estate

**Job two. Death turns over capital.**

**Argument:** Inheritance is the largest redistributive event in a capitalist economy, and it happens automatically, universally, and without anyone passing a law. Death is the only force that reliably breaks up a concentrated fortune.

**The consequence:** Piketty's r greater than g describes a slow drift, and the reason it stays slow is that compounding keeps getting interrupted. By transfer, by division among heirs, by taxes, and by the incompetence of the third generation. Remove death and the interruptions stop. Capital compounds forever inside one unchanging decision maker with one unchanging appetite for risk. The slow drift becomes a fixed point.

**The legal anchor:** The rule against perpetuities is a centuries old legal device whose entire purpose is to force turnover, to stop the dead from controlling property forever. Several US states have now abolished or gutted it, which is what made perpetual dynasty trusts possible. We have already started dismantling our anti permanence machinery, for tax reasons, without noticing what it was for.

**Evidence needed:** Piketty on inheritance flows as a share of national income. The perpetual trust jurisdictions and the legal scholarship on the rule against perpetuities. Corporate mortality data, since firm turnover in the S&P is the institutional version of the same mechanism.

---

## 5. The Funeral Principle

**Job three. Death turns over ideas.**

**Argument:** Max Planck's line about science advancing one funeral at a time is not cynicism. It is an empirical claim, and it has been tested.

**The evidence:** Azoulay, Fons-Rosen and Graff Zivin looked at what happens to a research field after a star scientist dies unexpectedly. Outsiders move in, publication patterns shift, and the direction of the field changes. The effect is measurable. Incumbency in ideas is enforced by living people occupying the seats that confer authority, and it ends when they vacate them.

**The consequence:** Immortal incumbents produce intellectual lock in. Not through censorship, but through the ordinary functioning of tenure, editorial boards, grant committees and prestige. Then combine it with chapter 4. The same people hold the capital and the ideas, permanently, and each reinforces the other.

**Evidence needed:** Azoulay et al., "Does Science Advance One Funeral at a Time?" Kuhn on paradigm succession. Planck's original passage in context.

## 6. Nobody Goes to Space. DRAFTED.

**Job four. Death prices risk.**

**Argument:** The value of a statistical life rises with wealth and with years remaining, and longevity raises both without limit. A person who does not age is a perpetual bond, so their entire value collapses onto one question: the chance of an accident. Death has no exit, so over a long enough horizon any yearly chance of dying makes ruin certain. The only rational policy left is continuous risk avoidance at almost any cost.

**The paradox:** Ability and willingness are not two forces in tension. They are one force running in opposite directions. Every civilization rich enough to reach the stars is too rich to go.

**The turn:** The frontier belongs to whoever can afford to die there. The poor, the artificial, and those who decline longevity on purpose.

## 7. Vacancy

**Job five. Death creates openings.**

**Argument:** Careers are queues, and queues move because people leave them. Every promotion is somebody's exit. Harrison White's vacancy chain theory models this directly. An opening at the top propagates downward through an organization, and each move creates the next opening. The chain starts with a departure.

**The consequence:** No exits means no entry. A permanently staffed hierarchy does not have a slower ladder. It has no ladder. The young inherit a structurally closed system, and every position of authority is held by someone who intends to hold it indefinitely.

**The present tense evidence:** This one is already visible. Look at the age profile of the US Senate, of federal judgeships, of tenured faculty, of corporate boards. Gerontocracy is not a future scenario. It is a current trend line with the mortality term still fully intact. Remove the term and extrapolate.

**Evidence needed:** White, *Chains of Opportunity*, 1970. Current age distributions in legislatures, judiciaries and universities. The tenure and mandatory retirement literature. Youth unemployment in economies with blocked advancement.

## 8. The Last Chain Letter

**Argument:** Added after the first draft, in response to the objection that the book raises Samuelson as its opening move and never returns to him. The five jobs meet the public balance sheet here, and the overlapping generations model turns out to be the design document for every pay-as-you-go pension system rather than a curiosity.

**The hook:** The trustees of the American social insurance system publish a date every year. The fund pays in full until 2033 and 77 percent after that. The interesting question is not why it is short but why a national pension is the kind of object that can be short.

**The trap:** Blanchard 1985 says lower mortality lowers the rate. Blanchard 2019 says a rate below the growth rate makes public debt free. Read in sequence they suggest longevity solves the public finances. The error is that the low rate and the pension promise have the same cause.

**The case:** Japan, worked rather than named. Not a delayed crisis but a closed accounting loop, in which the holders of the debt and the beneficiaries of the promise are the same institutions.

**What breaks:** Not default. Rigidity. Every unfunded promise converts future discretion into fixed obligation, and under mortality those obligations expire on a schedule.

# PART III. THE COUNTERPARTY

## 9. The Forkable Worker

**Argument:** Artificial agents are the first economic actors in history with genuinely arbitrary lifespans. They can be copied, paused, resumed, branched and retired at almost no cost. And, this is the operative part, at almost no moral cost.

**The consequence:** Every job I have attributed to death is a job that can be performed deliberately on an artificial agent. Capital turnover, idea turnover, vacancy, risk bearing. The mortality we are removing from people becomes available as a design setting somewhere else. This is the chapter where the book stops being about longevity and becomes about the pairing.

**The unresolved question, left explicitly unresolved:** whether that moral cost stays negligible. The argument does not depend on the answer. The book is not credible if it declines to raise the question.

**Evidence needed:** How agent systems actually work. Spawning, checkpointing, retirement, described concretely rather than speculatively. This chapter needs the fewest citations and the most precision.

---

## 10. The Mortal Class

**Argument:** Put chapters 6 and 8 together. People become a permanent, risk averse class that holds the capital. Artificial agents become the mortal class that takes the risk, absorbs the churn and settles the frontier.

**The problem this creates:** Presence becomes title. Historically, whoever bears the cost of establishing a claim ends up owning it. That is what homesteading, salvage and discovery doctrines all encode. If the things establishing a presence on the frontier are not the things that paid for it, then ownership is an open question, and it is the political question of the next two centuries rather than a technicality.

**The honest counterweight:** This is where a sharp reader will object that I have smuggled in assumptions about how capable AI becomes. Address it head on. The argument should hold at modest capability, and it should be stated at that level.

**Evidence needed:** Property doctrines of discovery, occupation and salvage. The Outer Space Treaty and its unresolved property provisions. The principal and agent literature for the ownership problem.

# PART IV. THE FROZEN WORLD

## 11. The Serrata

**Argument:** The synthesis chapter. What all five removals look like operating at once.

**The historical anchor, and the best one available:** In 1297 Venice closed its Great Council to any family not already inside it. The Serrata, the closing. The city's elite was frozen by law. Venice at that moment was the most sophisticated commercial society in Europe, with the most advanced financial instruments in the world. It did not collapse. It went static, comfortable and slowly irrelevant, and it took centuries to do it.

That is the right model for what this book describes. Not dystopia. Not collapse. A wealthy, safe, beautifully preserved civilization in which nothing further happens.

**The tone:** Refuse catastrophism. The frozen world is a pleasant place to live if you are already inside it. That is exactly what makes it hard to argue against and easy to drift into.

**Evidence needed:** Puga and Trefler on Venice and the Serrata. Acemoglu and Robinson's treatment of it. The economic history of Venetian decline.

# PART V. DESIGNED TURNOVER

## 12. Reinventing the Funeral

**Argument:** The constructive core. If death was doing five jobs and death is going away, the jobs still have to get done. Deliberately, by institution, in the open. Each of the five gets a proposed mechanism, and each proposal gets its strongest objection printed next to it.

* **Discount rate.** How to price permanent assets when the rate approaches zero.
* **Capital turnover.** Reviving and generalizing the rule against perpetuities. Sunset clauses on ownership. Term limits on concentrated positions.
* **Idea turnover.** Rotating authority rather than people. Fixed terms on editorial, grant making and appointment power.
* **Risk.** Who is allowed to bear it, and how a society decides that once every death is preventable.
* **Vacancy.** Term limits as an economic instrument rather than a political one.

**The framing that matters:** None of this is utopian. It is maintenance. We are removing a beam that was holding up the building, and the argument is only that something has to go in its place before we pull it out.

---

## 13. Terminal Value

**Argument:** The close. Terminal value is the finance term for everything beyond the forecast horizon, the number that breaks when the horizon becomes infinite. Come back to it explicitly and land the double meaning the title has been carrying for 120 pages.

**The closing turn:** Every generation until now has been forced into a relationship with the future by the certainty of not being in it. Cathedrals, constitutions, planted trees and children are all decisions made by people who knew they would not collect. We are about to become the first generation that might collect. The question the book ends on is whether a civilization that expects to be present for its own future is still capable of building one.

**Land on:** Longevity buys you Earth. The stars go to whoever is still willing to die.
